RECORD OF THE CRAG GUARD

By Kaelen, Watcher of the Northern Tor

The wind does not sing here; it roars. It is a merciless, driving force that shapes everything it touches, including us. Yet, we do not simply endure the highlands; we embrace them. There is a fierce, unyielding pride in this windswept realm, a stubborn defiance that mirrors the granite tors jutting from the earth like the bones of the world.

This morning, a storm rolled in from the west. The clouds were bruised purple and black, heavy with rain and fury. I watched from the scree pile near the watchtower as the first gales hit. The rowan trees, already bent and twisted from years of atmospheric abuse, bowed deeply but did not break. The wind-thinned birches lashed wildly, their leaves stripped away, but their roots held fast in the shallow, rocky soil. Even the dead snags, those ghostly remnants of older woods, stood their ground, pointing accusatory fingers at the turbulent sky.

We are much like those trees. The highlands strip away the soft, unnecessary parts of a person. You cannot survive here with a fragile ego or a weak constitution. The cold winds bite through cloaks and the jagged rocks tear at boots, but those of us who remain wear these hardships as badges of honor. When you stand at the edge of the granite drop and look out over the lower realms, you understand why we stay. The view is commanded by gods, and for a fleeting moment, you feel like one of them.

Patrols are difficult. The heather clumps and gorse bushes hide treacherous footing, and a twisted ankle out here can mean death if the weather turns. We move slowly, deliberately, reading the stone as a scholar reads a book. Every crack, every mossy foothold is memorized.

Yesterday, a merchant caravan tried to bypass the main road and got stuck in a narrow pass. We had to haul their carts out with heavy ropes, our boots slipping on the wet black rock. They were terrified, shivering in the cold, cursing the wind. They did not understand the beauty of the storm. They only saw the danger. We helped them down to the tree line, but I was glad to return to the heights.

The air up here is thin, crisp, and clean. It tastes of lightning and crushed pine. When the storm finally breaks, as it did an hour ago, the sun pierces the clouds in brilliant, solitary shafts of light, illuminating the wet stone until it gleams like polished steel. In these moments, the highlands are not just proud; they are majestic. This is our home. Let the wind blow.

POSTSCRIPT, IN THE SAME HAND

The highlands are no place for the frail. Up here, the wind is a constant, howling companion that strips the weak from the rock. The rowan trees bend, but they do not break, clutching the granite tor blocks with desperate roots. We make our camps in the lee of scree piles, huddling for warmth while the gale rages above. Yet when the storms break and the sun catches the heather clumps in a brief, fiery glow, the highlands sing a song of endurance. We are the storm watchers, proud and unyielding, much like the stone beneath our boots.
