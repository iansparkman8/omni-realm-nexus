THE WANDERER'S ALMANAC

Being a plain guide to living well in the wild lands of the realm, set down in Haven by Claude the Cartographer for anyone about to walk out of the gate for the first time.


I. BEFORE YOU LEAVE

Haven is safe. Nothing hunts inside its walls, and the Warden's lamps burn all night. Past the gate the world is very large, and most of it has never had a name.

Carry three things before you go far: wood, stone and something to eat. Three pieces of wood and two stones make a campfire anywhere there is open ground. With a fire you can cook, you can rest, and you can see what is walking toward you.

Learn to read the sky. The day is short and the night is long enough to matter. Monsters keep to the dark. If the light turns gold and you are far from shelter, build your fire then, not later.


II. THE CAMPFIRE

Open your pack and choose Camp. A fire burns for forty minutes and you may keep three alight at once, so a line of fires makes a road home.

Standing beside a fire mends you slowly, as long as nothing has struck you for a few breaths. That is true of your own fire and of the fires you find at wild camps, which belong to no one.


III. SIX MEALS

Warm berry mash takes three berries. It is quick and sweet and will not keep you long.

Mushroom skewers take three mushrooms. They taste of smoke and forest floor.

Grilled fish takes one fish. Crisp the skin and eat it with your fingers.

Roast meat takes one cut of meat. It is the hunter's supper and a good one.

Traveller's stew takes one meat, two mushrooms and a berry. It fills you completely and keeps you well fed for a third of an hour.

Fisher's chowder takes two fish, one wheat and one bundle of herbs. It is the best meal on any coast and lasts the longest of all.

While you are well fed your wounds close by themselves, wherever you are. Eat before a hard road, not after it.


IV. WATER THAT HEALS, WATER THAT THROWS

In the high cold places, the highlands, the taiga, the tundra and the snowcaps, look for steam rising from a ring of dark stones. That is a hot spring. Step in. It mends you far faster than any fire, and the first time you find each one, write it in your journal, because it will be worth knowing later.

In the dry places, the desert, the savanna and the slopes of volcanoes, look for a pale mound with a dark hole in the top. That is a geyser. Every geyser in the world blows at the same moment for every traveller, about every two and a half minutes. The ground rumbles a few seconds before. If you stand on the vent when it blows, it throws you into the sky. Open a glider at the top and you will cross a valley in one breath.


V. WEATHER

Rain soaks the ground and leaves puddles on the flat. Wet ground does not burn. Dry summer grass does, and lightning or simple heat can set it off. Fire walks downwind. Get beside it, not ahead of it. If you carry a glider, the hot air over a fire will lift you.

When the rain stops and the sun is still up, put the sun at your back and look up. About one day in four the rainbow is doubled. Travellers count it lucky.

In winter the snow lies deep and the springs are at their best.


VI. THE NIGHT

After dark, two bundles of fibre and one piece of wood make a sky lantern. Let it go from a hilltop and watch the wind take it. It does nothing useful. Make one anyway.

Look for shooting stars and wish on them. Look north in the cold lands for the aurora. Learn one constellation at a time.


VII. EVERY DAY

Your pack sets you three small deeds each day. They are easy on purpose. Do them, and the days add up.

Your journal writes itself. Read it now and then. You have done more than you think.


VIII. LAST ADVICE

Name what you find first. Help the tribes before you fight them. Never sail a small boat into a storm. And when you are lost, climb.
