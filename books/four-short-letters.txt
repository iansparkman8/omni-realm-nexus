FOUR LETTERS FROM THE WILD

Collected in Haven's library from travellers who wrote home. Each is short; each is honest.

THE DEEP ROOTS
By Ranger Thorne, of the forest

The forest is an ancient, breathing thing. Step beneath the canopy, and the world of the Haven fades into myth. Here, time is measured not in hours, but in the slow growth of the deep-rooted oaks and the creeping spread of fern bushes across the shaded floor. It is a place of shadows and filtered light, where every rustling leaf feels like a whispered secret.

We walked among the towering trunks, the air thick with the scent of damp earth and moss. The forest does not welcome you, but neither does it cast you out. It simply endures. One must learn to move quietly here, to respect the ancient silence.

I have seen the great beech trees, their smooth, grey bark standing like pillars of a forgotten temple. The forest demands humility. It is easy to lose your way among the shifting shadows and identical trunks. Yet, there is a profound beauty in its tangled depths: a sense of wild, untamed life that persists in the dark.

For those who know how to listen, the forest is a sanctuary. For those who do not, it is a labyrinth. Step carefully.

TIDES AND TIME
By Mariner Sol, of the coast

Clear skies, bright sun, and the endless rhythm of the tide. The beach is a place of perpetual motion, yet it brings a profound stillness to the soul. Here, the land surrenders to the ocean, offering up leaning palms and driftwood logs as tributes to the salt and foam.

We walk the shoreline, our boots leaving temporary marks in the wet sand before the sea washes them away. It is a humbling reminder that all things pass. The coastal pines stand like sentinels against the horizon, their needles singing in the sea breeze.

There is joy to be found in the clear, bright shallows, in the salt spray that coats our skin and the barnacled boulders that guard the coves. The ocean is vast and unknowable, but the beach is its welcoming edge. We are transient guests here, resting briefly before setting sail once more.

MERCILESS SANDS
By an unknown traveller, of the desert

Water. It is the only thought that survives in this bleached, merciless wasteland. The desert strips away everything unnecessary: pride, hope, and eventually, life. The sun beats down like a hammer upon an anvil, baking the sandstone hoodoos into cracked, lifeless monuments.

We move only at night, when the burning heat subsides to a biting chill. The landscape is a sea of dunes, dotted only by the twisted forms of saguaro cacti and dry tumble shrubs. To lose your way here is to surrender to the sand.

There is a terrible beauty in the starkness, in the wind-carved rocks that stand as silent witnesses to the sun's wrath. But do not be fooled by the stillness. The desert gives nothing and takes everything. Tread lightly, and bring more water than you think you need.

THE SILENT PEAK
By Vorn, high-altitude guide, of the snowcaps

Silence. It is the first thing you notice when you ascend into the snowcap. It is not the peaceful silence of the meadows, but a severe, crushing silence that presses against your ears. Here, the world is buried under a suffocating blanket of white, and the frost-killed snags stand like skeletal warnings against the grey sky.

The cold here is an entity. It seeks out the gaps in your clothing, freezing your breath into rime on your scarf. The snow-laden spruce trees bow under the weight of winter, a heavy burden they carry until the rare thaws.

Survival here demands respect for the mountain. One careless step on an ice-split rock, one shout to disturb the silent snowcaps, can bring the mountain down upon you. It is a severe, unforgiving place, yet to stand at its peak is to touch the sky. It is worth the climb, if only to prove that you can.
